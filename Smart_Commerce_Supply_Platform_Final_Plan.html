Final Project Document

Smart Commerce & Supply Platform

B2B + B2C + Logistics + Advertising + Data & AI

Vision: Transform the local trade supply chain from relationship-driven communication and manual ordering into a trusted digital network connecting suppliers, retailers, consumers, delivery, and data in one platform.

Central strategic recommendation: Launch B2B first in a defined geographic market and product category. Add delivery and settlement capabilities next, then expand into B2C, advertising, and data/AI only after repeat transactions and sustainable unit economics are demonstrated.
Prepared as: Strategic, technical, product, operational, and go-to-market planning document
Date: September 2026

Executive Summary

The proposed project is a multi-sided digital platform for local trade, procurement, and distribution. It starts with the wholesale market, connecting wholesalers with retailers, and then expands progressively into delivery, payments, consumer commerce, digital advertising, analytics, and artificial intelligence. The objective is not to build another online store, but to create digital infrastructure for local commerce.

The platform's core value comes from combining four needs in one environment: supplier and product discovery, B2B purchasing, delivery execution and tracking, and the use of transaction data to improve purchasing, selling, pricing, and promotion decisions.

The project should not launch all capabilities in a single release. It should be delivered in consecutive phases, with Phase 1 producing a commercially usable product that can be deployed to real merchants. Every later phase should be conditional on measurable growth, usage, operating readiness, and unit economics.

What differentiates the project?

  • B2B wholesale-to-retail marketplace with quantity pricing, promotions, purchasing, RFQ, and negotiation.
  • A delivery network that can be operated by the merchant, trusted independent drivers, or logistics partners.
  • Merchant and driver verification, ratings, reputation, and trust scoring.
  • Personalized content and offers based on behavior, location, interests, and transaction history.
  • A data and business-intelligence engine that can later support demand forecasting, smart reorder, and price intelligence.
  • An advertising platform that turns the marketplace into a sales and marketing channel, not merely a transaction channel.

Guiding implementation principle

The architecture should be scalable from day one, but the first release must not attempt to solve every future requirement. The recommended starting point is a Modular Monolith with clear domain boundaries, PostgreSQL + PostGIS, Flutter for mobile, Next.js for web, NestJS + TypeScript for backend services, Redis for caching/temporary state, WebSockets for real-time functions, and integrations for maps and notifications.

3. Problem Statement and Value Proposition

3.1 Wholesaler problems

  • Limited reach beyond the existing relationship network.
  • Manual updates of price lists and promotions through phone calls and messaging.
  • Weak visibility into real demand, active areas, and fast-moving categories.
  • Orders, delivery, and collections managed through disconnected tools.
  • Difficulty building a recurring digital channel that brings customers back.

3.2 Retailer problems

  • Manual supplier discovery and price comparison.
  • Limited visibility into actual stock, promotions, and availability.
  • Repeated ordering through phone calls or messaging.
  • High time cost of collecting goods from multiple suppliers.
  • Weak historical data about purchases and prices.

3.3 Consumer problems

  • Difficulty discovering nearby stores and their available products.
  • Unclear offers, prices, and availability.
  • Need to visit or contact multiple stores.

4. Business Model and Commerce Ecosystem

4.1 Multi-sided model

The platform evolves from a B2B marketplace into a multi-sided local commerce network. Each side increases the value of the others: more suppliers improve selection; more buyers make registration more attractive for suppliers; a reliable delivery network reduces friction; and more transactions generate data that can improve conversion, sales, and advertising revenue.

4.2 Revenue engines

Transaction commissionPercentage of order value or category-specific flat fee.Medium at launch, high later
Merchant subscriptionFree / Pro / Business / Enterprise plans.High after value is proven
Delivery feesService and delivery fees with a platform share where the network is operated by the platform.Medium
AdvertisingSponsored Products / Stores / Offers / Search Ads.High after audience scale
Data servicesReports, trends, pricing, and market indicators for merchants.Later
Enterprise integrationsERP, API, and advanced integrations for larger organizations.Later

4.3 Unit economics

Economics must be measured per order and per merchant, not only as total revenue. Important measures include GMV, average order value, platform contribution per order, delivery cost, payment fee, support cost, CAC, and repeat-order rate. These measures determine whether expansion creates value or simply increases losses.

Launch pricing principle: Avoid a high entry barrier. Make joining and discovering the platform easy. Use a temporary commission waiver or introductory plans to reduce trial friction, then introduce monetization after repeat usage is demonstrated.

6.2 Quantity pricing

Tiered pricing must be supported from the start because B2B does not operate as a single-price retail catalog. Prices may differ by quantity, customer group, promotion, geography, or contract. The actual agreed price at order completion must also be preserved for auditability.

6.3 Promotion engine

  • Percentage or fixed-amount discount.
  • Buy X Get Y.
  • Quantity-based discounts.
  • Time-limited promotion.
  • First-order offer.
  • Offer for a customer segment.
  • Geographic offer.
  • Platform-exclusive offer.
  • Bundles and product baskets.

6.4 Bulk import

Bulk Import is an adoption requirement, not a secondary convenience. Merchants should be able to import Excel/CSV files, preview results, validate errors, and publish safely. Later, OCR/AI can extract product data from invoices and supplier price lists.

Merchant UX principle: every manual data-entry step should have a faster path where possible: Excel import, copy from an existing product, duplicate a list, import an image/invoice, or accept an automatic suggestion.

7.2 Master Order / Sub Orders

As the marketplace grows, one purchase may contain products from multiple merchants. Use a Master Order representing the buyer's purchase intent, with separate Sub Orders per supplier. This simplifies settlement, delivery, returns, commissions, and status management.

7.3 Request for Quotation (RFQ)

RFQ allows a retailer to publish a bulk purchase request and receive offers from multiple wholesalers. It turns the platform from a catalog into a competitive procurement marketplace and is particularly useful for large, recurring, contract-based, or price-sensitive purchases.

7.4 Counter-offer negotiation

The buyer can propose a price for a specified quantity and the merchant can accept, reject, or issue a counter-offer. The complete negotiation history should be retained as an unambiguous record for future reference and dispute resolution.

8. Delivery and Tracking

8.1 Fulfillment models

  • Self-pickup from wholesaler warehouse/store.
  • Delivery by wholesaler employee.
  • Delivery by a trusted driver linked to the merchant.
  • Delivery by an independent driver in the platform network.
  • Delivery by a partner logistics company.

8.2 Driver selection

The dispatch engine should consider distance, ETA, driver availability, vehicle capacity/type, rating, success rate, service zone, task cost, and current workload. Rules can be simple in the first release, then evolve into an optimization engine once sufficient delivery data exists.

8.3 Real-time tracking

The mobile app should send driver location using an adaptive update rate and show status, location, and ETA to the relevant users. GPS frequency should increase during an active trip and decrease when the driver is stationary, balancing tracking quality, battery consumption, and infrastructure cost.

8.4 Proof of Delivery

  • OTP or delivery code.
  • Recipient signature when required.
  • Delivery photo.
  • Date and time.
  • Geographic location.
  • Recipient name.

8.5 Geofencing

Later, geofencing can trigger events such as driver approaching, entering the delivery zone, arriving at the exact location, or exceeding the promised window. Treat these as first-class system events linked to orders, notifications, and analytics.

9. Trust, Ratings, and Disputes

9.1 Verification

The platform needs a clear verification path for merchants and delivery providers. Required documents depend on country and sector, so KYC/business verification should be configurable rather than hard-coded for one market.

9.2 Trust Score

The Trust Score must be explainable enough that it does not become an opaque punishment mechanism. Public-facing badges such as Verified Merchant, Trusted Merchant, and Fast Fulfillment can provide clear signals without exposing every detail of the ranking algorithm.

DimensionExample inputs
Order reliabilityCompletion rate, cancellations, disputes
PaymentOn-time payment behavior, overdue amounts
QualityCustomer ratings, product mismatch
OperationsResponse time, preparation time
DeliveryCarrier reliability, successful delivery
HistoryActivity duration and completed transactions

9.3 Disputes

Disputes should be attached to the order itself and support photos, video, proof of delivery, and conversation history. Suggested flow: Open dispute → collect evidence → request other party response → platform review → decision → settlement/closure.

9.4 Chat

Offer chat linked to an order rather than a generic messaging system. Order-linked messages support negotiation and coordination and later become useful evidence in dispute handling.

10. B2C Consumer Commerce Phase

B2C should follow a strong base of retailers and recurring orders. Launching it too early increases complexity and fragments the team. When launched, its purpose is to turn participating retail stores into digital points of sale that can accept consumer demand.

10.1 B2C capabilities

  • Discover nearby stores.
  • Product catalogs.
  • Search and categories.
  • Offers.
  • Cart and checkout.
  • Delivery or pickup selection.
  • Tracking.
  • Ratings.
  • Favorites.
  • Smart Reorder.

10.2 Subscription vs. commission

Retailers may be offered a choice of low fixed subscription, per-order commission, or a hybrid model. The best option should be determined from measured economics: order frequency, order value, and acquisition/servicing cost per store.

10.3 Smart Reorder for consumers

The system can learn the buying frequency of recurring products and recommend reordering around the expected replenishment date. This turns the app from an occasional shopping tool into a service with recurring utility.

11.2 Ad decision engine

The engine can balance relevance, quality, budget, competition, and probability of click/purchase, with controls that prevent unsuitable ads from reaching users. Record impressions, clicks, conversions, orders, revenue, and ROAS.

Important commercial principle: advertising must not destroy search quality. Product quality, relevance, and merchant trust must remain strong ranking factors alongside paid placement.

11.3 Personalization

Users should not all see the same home page. Ranking can consider location, behavior, purchase history, search history, preferred categories, typical price range, and interaction history. Start with transparent rules and move to ML ranking once enough data exists.

13. Advanced Strategic Capabilities

13.1 Smart Basket

Instead of optimizing each product independently, the platform can propose the lowest-cost or best-value basket across suppliers, considering unit price, minimum order, delivery cost, stock availability, supplier trust, and expected delivery time.

13.2 RFQ Marketplace

Retailers can publish large procurement needs and receive multiple supplier offers. This supports large orders, contracts, recurring procurement, and price competition.

13.3 Digital Procurement Lists

Retailers can save weekly or monthly procurement lists and execute them with one action, while suggested quantities are refreshed using stock levels and expected demand.

13.4 Market Intelligence

After marketplace scale is achieved, the platform can provide aggregated indicators for demand trends, average prices, emerging categories, and seasonality. Data must be aggregated/anonymized where required and handled consistently with contracts and local law.

13.5 Merchant Mini ERP

The platform can later expand into sales, inventory, expenses, customers, suppliers, and profitability. This makes the product part of daily merchant operations and increases retention.

15. UX and Conversion Strategy

15.1 UX principles

  • Minimize the steps required to complete an order.
  • Show the most relevant information first.
  • Clearly display price, quantity, and minimum order quantity.
  • Make delivery cost and arrival time transparent.
  • Provide reorder from previous orders.
  • Make order status persistent and easy to resume.
  • Support local language and full RTL where required.

15.2 Merchant activation

True wholesaler activation is not registration. It is: verified + sufficient products published + first order. Onboarding should be designed around this sequence, using progress indicators and direct assistance.

15.3 Retailer activation

True retailer activation is: registration + area selection + supplier discovery + first order (or meaningful saved supplier) + first completed purchase. Every screen before that milestone should reduce friction.

15.4 Non-intrusive notifications

Separate notifications into Transactional, Promotional, and Behavioral. Order-related notifications are operational; promotional notifications should follow consent and market rules. Personalization should increase relevance, not notification volume.

17. Data and Financial Model

17.1 Core entities

User, Organization, OrganizationMember, Role, Permission, Store, Warehouse, Category, Brand, Product, ProductVariant, InventoryItem, PriceList, PriceTier, Promotion, Cart, Order, OrderItem, Payment, Settlement, Delivery, Driver, DriverLocation, Review, Dispute, Conversation, Message, Campaign, Impression, Click, Notification, AuditLog.

17.2 Financial ledger

Do not rely on a single total field to represent an order financially. Separate product value, discount, tax where applicable, platform commission, delivery fee, payment fee, and the net payable to each party. Build a ledger with debit/credit entries and transaction references because multi-party settlement becomes essential as the platform scales.

17.3 Webhooks and reconciliation

A checkout confirmation page is not sufficient proof of payment. Payment success should be confirmed by a trusted provider callback/webhook, using idempotency and correlation IDs, then ledger entries should be created from a known payment state.

18. Security, Compliance, and Privacy

18.1 Key controls

  • HTTPS/TLS for all communications.
  • Encryption of sensitive data at rest where appropriate.
  • Short-lived JWTs + refresh token rotation.
  • RBAC and resource permissions at organization level.
  • Rate limiting and abuse prevention.
  • Audit logs for sensitive actions.
  • Secrets management; never store secrets in source control.
  • Backups and restoration testing.
  • Employee/support access policies.
  • API authorization checks on every protected resource.

18.2 Location privacy

Location is one of the most operationally sensitive datasets in the project. Collect it only when needed, define the purpose, and reduce precision or retention duration when the business need no longer requires it. Separate active delivery location from permanent historical tracking unless there is a clear operational or legal requirement.

18.3 Compliance

Identity, invoicing, tax, e-commerce, payments, consumer protection, and logistics requirements vary by country. Verification, tax, document, and policy rules should therefore be configurable by market, with local legal review before launching regulated financial or credit functionality.

Technical payment availability is not enough. The platform needs positive or improvable unit economics, transaction-cost visibility, meaningful repeat ordering, merchant satisfaction, and support processes capable of handling refunds and disputes.

24. Phase 4 - B2C Marketplace

24.1 Objectives

  • Turn existing retail stores into digital stores.
  • Acquire consumer traffic.
  • Add a new demand channel for retailers.
  • Build daily/weekly purchase loops in suitable categories.

24.2 Consumer features

Discover by location, search, categories, offers, cart, checkout, tracking, ratings, favorites, and Smart Reorder.

24.3 B2C launch strategy

Do not open B2C to every retailer simultaneously. Start with the most ready stores: clear assortment, reliable fulfillment, and realistic delivery coverage. Expand store count based on conversion and completion rates.

25. Phase 5 - Advertising and Analytics

25.1 Advertising MVP

  • Create campaign.
  • Select product/store.
  • Set budget.
  • Set duration.
  • Geo-targeting.
  • Select placements.
  • Report impressions, clicks, and orders.

25.2 Merchant analytics

  • Views.
  • Conversion.
  • Top products.
  • Sales over time.
  • Customer behavior.
  • Repeat-order rate.
  • Promotion performance.
  • Internal price comparison where permitted.

25.3 Gate to Phase 6

Do not scale advertising until there is enough audience and interaction volume to create real advertiser value. Merchants must have evidence of a real audience with meaningful purchase intent.

26. Phase 6 - AI and Optimization

26.1 Priority

Prioritize AI capabilities that produce measurable business outcomes: smart reorder, product entry, recommendations, forecasting, and anomaly detection.

26.2 AI rule

Every AI feature must have a KPI. Smart Reorder is measured by lift in repeat purchase rate; Product AI by time-to-publish a correct product; recommendations by CTR, conversion, and attributed revenue. Do not use AI simply because AI is available.

27. Phase 7 - Commerce Infrastructure

  • Structured geographic expansion.
  • Multiple branches and warehouses.
  • Advanced merchant employee roles and permissions.
  • ERP/API integrations.
  • Accounting integrations.
  • Delivery companies and fleet integrations.
  • Trade-credit partnerships through regulated financial partners.
  • Market intelligence.
  • Developer platform and partner APIs.
End-state vision: once merchants depend on the platform for procurement, inventory, orders, delivery, payments, and intelligence, the product becomes closer to a Commerce Operating System than a marketplace alone.

30. Quality and Testing

30.1 Test types

  • Unit tests for domain logic and modules.
  • Integration tests for orders, payments, notifications.
  • API contract tests.
  • End-to-end tests for critical flows.
  • Load/performance tests before scale expansion.
  • Security tests and dependency scanning.
  • Mobile device testing.
  • Usability testing with real merchants.

30.2 Critical scenarios

  • Merchant accepts an order, then inventory changes.
  • Multiple quantity price tiers.
  • Order changes after acceptance according to policy.
  • Payment failure and retry.
  • Duplicate payment webhook.
  • Driver failure during delivery.
  • Weak mobile connectivity.
  • WebSocket interruption.
  • Dispute after proof of delivery.
  • Partial cancellation in a multi-supplier order.

30.3 Engineering principle

Every financial event and order-state change must be traceable and reconstructable from logs. Sensitive operations should be idempotent when the same request arrives more than once.

31. Operations and Customer Service

31.1 Pre-scale operating functions

  • Merchant onboarding.
  • Verification.
  • Catalog assistance.
  • Order support.
  • Delivery operations.
  • Payment reconciliation.
  • Dispute handling.
  • Fraud review.

31.2 Operational SLA

Define response targets by issue type: order, payment, delivery, or account. Exact numbers should be set after market discovery, but response time, resolution time, and user satisfaction should be measured from day one.

31.3 Playbooks

Create a playbook for recurring incidents such as payment failure, missing order, missing item, late driver, unresponsive merchant, compromised account, dispute, or promotion abuse.

35. Phase 1 Actual Launch Plan

35.1 Before launch

  • Select a specific launch area.
  • Sign/align a group of anchor suppliers.
  • Build a real initial catalog.
  • Select a Pilot retailer group.
  • Train the operations team.
  • Test every critical ordering flow.
  • Prepare marketing materials.
  • Set up analytics and support.

35.2 First 30 days

  • Manually oversee every order where necessary.
  • Communicate daily with a small merchant group.
  • Record failure and drop-off reasons.
  • Remove any step that blocks the first order.
  • Collect real success stories.
  • Run small tests across multiple acquisition channels.

35.3 Days 31-90

  • Increase merchant count based on data.
  • Target adjacent areas with commercial density.
  • Add Bulk Import and Reorder early where useful.
  • Expand promotions and referral.
  • Start a delivery pilot when order volume justifies it.

35.4 Discipline rules

  • Do not expand geographically before retention is stable.
  • Do not spend heavily on broad advertising before acceptable CAC is known.
  • Do not add features unless they improve a KPI or reduce a clearly identified operating cost.
  • Do not open B2C broadly before retailer readiness is proven.

36. Competitive Moat

The interface can be copied. The accumulated network cannot be copied easily. The proposed moat has five connected layers: supplier density in target areas, real transaction data, trust and reputation history, delivery network, and data-driven procurement/intelligence tools.

Moat layerHow it compounds
Supply densityNumber of active suppliers and catalogs in targeted areas
Demand densityActive buyers and repeat orders
Trust graphTransaction history, ratings, and commitment
Logistics networkHistorical delivery time, cost, and driver capability data
Data intelligenceSearch, purchasing, pricing, and reorder behavior
Merchant lock-inInventory, orders, analytics, and integrations
Most important strategic decision: success will not come from the number of features. It will come from building real transaction density in a defined area, then using those transactions to build trust, logistics, data, and intelligence. At that point organic network effects become possible and expansion becomes less risky.

37. Product Governance and Decision-Making

37.1 Product Council

Review KPIs, churn/lost customers, order economics, risks, and proposed features on a regular cadence. Every major feature should have a hypothesis, KPI, cost estimate, and success/stop criterion.

37.2 Feature lifecycle

Idea → Discovery → Prototype → Pilot → Release → Measure → Improve / Kill

This prevents a large backlog of unused features and protects delivery speed and commercial focus.

37.3 Data governance

Define data-access responsibility, what merchants can see, and what must be aggregated or hidden. Do not sell or share sensitive merchant data in a way that reveals individual competitive intelligence.

38. Final Recommendations

38.1 Decisions recommended now

  1. Adopt a B2B-first vision rather than launching full B2B/B2C simultaneously.
  2. Select a local launch market and first category before full development.
  3. Use Flutter for mobile, Next.js for web, and NestJS for backend unless team constraints make another stack materially better.
  4. Use PostgreSQL + PostGIS as the data core and Redis for speed and temporary state.
  5. Use a Modular Monolith in Phase 1 with clear domain boundaries.
  6. Build analytics, auditability, and ledger-ready architecture from the start even if some modules are simplified in the MVP.
  7. Design Product, Order, and Payment APIs as independent contracts so B2C, logistics, and advertising can be added later.
  8. Treat merchant onboarding, catalog quality, and repeat ordering as higher priorities than feature count.
  9. Manage the project through KPI-linked phase gates, not a calendar alone.
